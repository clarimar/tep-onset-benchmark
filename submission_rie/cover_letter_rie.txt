Dear Editor,

We submit for your consideration the manuscript "A Labeling Artifact Bounds
Reported Accuracy on the Tennessee Eastman Benchmark", transferred from
Computers & Chemical Engineering.

Benchmarks are how engineering disciplines compare methods, and a benchmark
carries assumptions that are inherited by every study that uses it. This
manuscript documents one such assumption in the Tennessee Eastman Process, the
most widely used benchmark for fault detection and diagnosis in industrial
process monitoring, and shows that it bounds what any study can report on that
benchmark, independently of the method being evaluated.

Each fault is injected 160 samples into every 960-sample official test run, so
observations preceding injection carry a fault label while describing nominal
operation. Under the labels distributed with the dataset this caps the recall
of every fault class at 800/960 = 0.8333, and the cap binds: faults 6 and 7
attain 0.8333 and 0.8332, the ceiling itself. Rescoring a frozen XGBoost model
over the post-onset segment of the complete official test partition, 10.08
million observations and no refitting, raises macro-F1 from 0.7200 to 0.8054,
accuracy from 0.6754 to 0.7949 and MCC from 0.6621 to 0.7852. Applying the same
protocol to the validation partition turns an apparent validation-to-test
generalization gap of +0.0483 in macro-F1 into -0.0125, showing that the
reported gap reflects the difference in mislabeled fraction between partitions
rather than a loss of generalization.

The effect belongs to the labeling convention, not to any estimator. Across
seven classifiers spanning a single decision tree, four ensembles and two
linear baselines, the relative accuracy gain lies between 0.142 and 0.150
against a structural bound of 0.1587 derived from the retained fraction of the
partition. For calibration of its weight: quadrupling the training data gains
0.0086 in post-onset macro-F1, an order of magnitude less than the convention.

We further document that three studies on the same simulator adopt three
different conventions, and we trace the divergence to a transition rather than
to oversight. In binary detection the pre-onset segment has a natural
destination in the normal class, and the false-alarm rate is defined over
precisely that segment, so the convention is built into the evaluation. In
multiclass diagnosis the same move alters the prior of the normal condition,
and the distinction disappears without ever being posed as a choice.

We believe the manuscript suits Results in Engineering. Its subject is not a
new method but the measurement practice surrounding an established engineering
benchmark, and the finding applies across the methods evaluated on it: the
classical classifiers reported here are instruments of demonstration rather
than the contribution. The manuscript reports a complete leakage-controlled
protocol, the computational cost of all six model families, and a learning
curve bounding the effect of training set size, so that the comparison between
the labeling artifact and the ordinary sources of variation is explicit. Two of
the authors work in industrial automation, and the discussion addresses what
the result implies for engineers who rely on published benchmark figures when
specifying diagnostic systems.

The benchmark follows a leakage-controlled protocol: partitioning by complete
simulation runs, standardization fitted on training data only, hyperparameter
selection isolated from the test partition, five-seed repeated validation with
Friedman and Nemenyi analysis, and a single frozen evaluation. All code, split
manifests, frozen hyperparameters, predictions and metrics are publicly
available at https://github.com/clarimar/tep-onset-benchmark and archived at
doi:10.5281/zenodo.22645013.

The manuscript is original, has not been published elsewhere and is not under
consideration by another journal. All authors have approved the submission and
declare no competing interests. Our use of AI-assisted tools in preparing the
manuscript is declared in the paper, before the reference list.
