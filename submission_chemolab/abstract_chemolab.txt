Each fault in the Tennessee Eastman Process (TEP) benchmark is injected 160 samples into every 960-sample test run, so observations preceding injection carry a fault label while describing nominal operation. We show that this convention caps the recall of every fault class at 800/960=0.8333, and that the cap binds: faults 6 and 7 attain 0.8333 and 0.8332, the ceiling itself. Rescoring a frozen gradient-boosting model over the post-onset segment of the complete official test partition, 10.08 million observations and no refitting, raises macro-F1 from 0.7200 to 0.8054 and reverses an apparent validation-to-test generalization gap of +0.0483 to -0.0125. Across seven classifiers spanning a single decision tree, four ensembles and two linear baselines, the relative accuracy gain lies between 0.142 and 0.150 against a structural bound of 0.1587, so the effect belongs to the labeling convention rather than to any estimator; for comparison, quadrupling the training data gains 0.0086. Three published studies on the same simulator adopt three different conventions, and we trace the divergence to the transition from binary detection, where the false-alarm rate is defined over the pre-onset segment, to multiclass diagnosis, where the distinction disappears. These results rest on a leakage-controlled benchmark of six classical classifiers partitioned by complete simulation runs, and they bound what any observation-level method evaluated on this benchmark can report.
