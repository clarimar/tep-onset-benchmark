Dear Editor,

We submit for your consideration the manuscript "A Labeling Artifact Bounds
Reported Accuracy on the Tennessee Eastman Benchmark".

The Tennessee Eastman Process is one of the most widely used public benchmarks
for multivariate process monitoring, and results obtained on it are routinely
compared across studies and across method families. We show that a labeling
convention embedded in the benchmark itself bounds what any observation-level
method can report on it, quantify that bound, and demonstrate that it is
independent of the estimator.

Each fault is injected 160 samples into every 960-sample official test run, so
observations preceding injection carry a fault label while describing nominal
operation. Under the labels distributed with the dataset this caps the recall
of every fault class at 800/960 = 0.8333, and the cap binds: faults 6 and 7
attain 0.8333 and 0.8332, the ceiling itself. Rescoring a frozen
gradient-boosting model over the post-onset segment of the complete official
test partition, 10.08 million observations and no refitting, raises macro-F1
from 0.7200 to 0.8054, accuracy from 0.6754 to 0.7949 and the Matthews
correlation coefficient from 0.6621 to 0.7852. Applying the same protocol to
the validation partition turns an apparent validation-to-test generalization
gap of +0.0483 in macro-F1 into -0.0125, so the reported gap reflects the
difference in mislabeled fraction between partitions rather than a loss of
generalization.

The effect belongs to the convention rather than to any estimator. Across seven
classifiers spanning a single decision tree, four ensembles and two linear
baselines, the relative accuracy gain lies between 0.142 and 0.150 against a
structural bound of 0.1587 derived from the retained fraction of the partition.
For calibration of its weight: quadrupling the training data gains 0.0086 in
post-onset macro-F1, an order of magnitude less than the convention. We further
document that three published studies on the same simulator adopt three
different conventions, and trace the divergence to the transition from binary
detection, where the false-alarm rate is defined over the pre-onset segment, to
multiclass diagnosis, where the distinction disappears without being posed as a
choice.

We believe the manuscript fits Chemometrics and Intelligent Laboratory Systems.
The journal has published foundational work on this benchmark, including the
comparison of principal component analysis, discriminant partial least squares
and Fisher discriminant analysis by Chiang, Russell and Braatz (2000) and the
canonical variate analysis study by Russell, Chiang and Braatz (2000), both
cited here. Our contribution is methodological rather than algorithmic: it
concerns how a widely used dataset is labeled and how performance on it should
be measured. The bound we derive is a property of the data and the scoring
rule, so it applies to any observation-level method evaluated against this
benchmark, latent-variable and machine-learning methods alike, and not only to
the classical classifiers we use as instruments of demonstration.

The supporting benchmark follows a leakage-controlled protocol: partitioning by
complete simulation runs rather than by observation, standardization fitted on
training data only, hyperparameter selection isolated from the test partition,
five-seed repeated validation with Friedman and Nemenyi analysis, run-level
bootstrap intervals, and a single frozen evaluation. A learning curve bounds
the effect of training set size, and the computational cost of all six model
families is reported. All code, split manifests, frozen hyperparameters,
predictions and metrics are publicly available at
https://github.com/clarimar/tep-onset-benchmark and archived at
doi:10.5281/zenodo.22645013. The Tennessee Eastman data are not redistributed
by us; they are available from Harvard Dataverse (doi:10.7910/DVN/6C3JR1).

The manuscript is original, has not been published elsewhere and is not under
consideration by another journal. All authors have approved the submission and
declare no competing interests. Our use of AI-assisted tools in preparing the
manuscript is declared in the paper, before the reference list.
