Dear Editor,

We submit for your consideration the manuscript "A Labeling Artifact Bounds
Reported Accuracy on the Tennessee Eastman Benchmark".

The Tennessee Eastman Process is one of the most widely used benchmarks for
data-driven process monitoring, and results obtained on it are routinely
compared across studies. We show that a property of the benchmark itself, not
of any classifier, bounds what those results can report, and that the
convention responsible is not applied consistently in the literature.

Each fault is injected 160 samples into every 960-sample official test run, so
observations preceding injection carry a fault label while describing nominal
operation. Under literal labeling this caps the recall of every fault class at
800/960 = 0.8333, and the cap binds: faults 6 and 7 attain 0.8333 and 0.8332,
the ceiling itself. Rescoring a frozen XGBoost model over the post-onset
segment of the complete official test partition, 10.08 million observations and
no refitting, raises macro-F1 from 0.7200 to 0.8054, accuracy from 0.6754 to
0.7949 and MCC from 0.6621 to 0.7852. Applying the same protocol to the
validation partition turns an apparent validation-to-test generalization gap of
+0.0483 in macro-F1 into -0.0125, so the reported gap reflects the difference in
mislabeled fraction between partitions rather than a loss of generalization.

The effect belongs to the convention rather than to any estimator. Across seven
classifiers spanning a single decision tree, four ensembles and two linear
baselines, the relative accuracy gain lies between 0.142 and 0.150 against a
structural bound of 0.1587 derived from the retained fraction of the partition.
For calibration: quadrupling the training data gains 0.0086 in post-onset
macro-F1, an order of magnitude less than the convention.

We also document that three studies on the same simulator adopt three different
conventions, and we trace the divergence to a transition rather than to
oversight. In binary detection the pre-onset segment has a natural destination
in the normal class, and the false-alarm rate is defined over precisely that
segment, so the convention is built into the evaluation. In multiclass
diagnosis the same move alters the prior of the normal condition, and the
distinction disappears without being posed as a choice.

We believe the manuscript fits Chemometrics and Intelligent Laboratory Systems.
The journal has published foundational work on this benchmark, including the
comparison of Fisher discriminant analysis, discriminant partial least squares
and principal component analysis by Chiang, Russell and Braatz (2000) and the
canonical variate analysis study by Russell, Chiang and Braatz (2000), both
cited here. The contribution is methodological: it concerns how a widely used
dataset is labeled and how classifier performance on it should be measured,
which bears on any chemometric method evaluated against it, not only on the
classical classifiers we use as instruments.

The benchmark supporting these results follows a leakage-controlled protocol:
partitioning by complete simulation runs, standardization fitted on training
data only, hyperparameter selection isolated from the test partition, five-seed
repeated validation with Friedman and Nemenyi analysis, and a single frozen
evaluation. All code, split manifests, frozen hyperparameters, predictions and
metrics are publicly available at https://github.com/clarimar/tep-onset-benchmark
and archived at doi:10.5281/zenodo.22645013.

The manuscript is original, has not been published elsewhere and is not under
consideration by another journal. All authors have approved the submission and
