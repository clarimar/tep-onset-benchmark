Dear Editor,

We submit for your consideration the manuscript "A Labeling Artifact Bounds
Reported Accuracy on the Tennessee Eastman Benchmark" as an Original Research
article.

Following the journal's requirement that the abstract specify both components,
we state them here as well. The engineering application is multiclass fault
diagnosis in industrial chemical processes, evaluated on the Tennessee Eastman
Process, the most widely used public benchmark for data-driven process
monitoring. The artificial intelligence contribution is an evaluation result:
we show that a labeling convention embedded in that benchmark bounds the
performance any supervised classifier can report on it, quantify the bound, and
demonstrate that it is independent of the learning algorithm.

Each fault is injected 160 samples into every 960-sample official test run, so
observations preceding injection carry a fault label while describing nominal
operation. Under the labels distributed with the dataset this caps the recall
of every fault class at 800/960 = 0.8333, and the cap binds: faults 6 and 7
attain 0.8333 and 0.8332, the ceiling itself. Rescoring a frozen gradient
boosting model over the post-onset segment of the complete official test
partition, 10.08 million observations and no refitting, raises macro-F1 from
0.7200 to 0.8054, accuracy from 0.6754 to 0.7949 and the Matthews correlation
coefficient from 0.6621 to 0.7852. Applying the same protocol to the validation
partition turns an apparent validation-to-test generalization gap of +0.0483 in
macro-F1 into -0.0125.

The effect belongs to the convention rather than to any estimator. Across seven
classifiers spanning a single decision tree, four ensembles and two linear
baselines, the relative accuracy gain lies between 0.142 and 0.150 against a
structural bound of 0.1587 derived from the retained fraction of the partition.
For calibration: quadrupling the training data gains 0.0086 in post-onset
macro-F1, an order of magnitude less than the convention. We further document
that three published studies on the same simulator adopt three different
conventions, and trace the divergence to the transition from binary detection,
where the false-alarm rate is defined over the pre-onset segment, to multiclass
diagnosis, where the distinction disappears.

The work is validated on a public dataset, as the journal requires: the
Tennessee Eastman simulation data are available from Harvard Dataverse
(doi:10.7910/DVN/6C3JR1) and are not redistributed by us. All code,
configuration files, run-level split manifests, frozen hyperparameters,
five-seed validation outputs, final predictions, class-level metrics, confusion
matrices, SHA-256 data hashes and the environment specification are deposited
in a public archive with a reserved DOI; the repository URL and the DOI
identify the authors and are therefore withheld from the anonymized manuscript,
and can be released to the editorial office on request.

The manuscript is formatted in single column, is 32 pages long, and the
abstract is 217 words. It does not propose a metaphor-based metaheuristic. Two
of the authors work in industrial automation, and the discussion addresses what
the result implies for engineers who rely on published benchmark figures when
specifying diagnostic systems.

The manuscript is original, has not been published elsewhere and is not under
consideration by another journal. All authors have approved the submission and
declare no competing interests. Our use of AI-assisted tools in preparing the
manuscript is declared on the title page and in the manuscript, before the
reference list.
